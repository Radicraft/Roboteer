The menu as a stage, and the garage that inherits it

The brief, in the owner's words: the bot hero menu looks inactive, sparse, badly designed and not dramatic enough, and the same design carries into the garage. The reference for look and layout is Gear.Club Stradale: your machine as the lit showroom hero with a camera that never quite stops moving, and the options as a calm horizontal row of tiles along the bottom. The quality bar is docs/graphics-bar.md, the house rules are docs/design-system.md, and the screens these replace are option M1 and option A of menus-options.html.

Every frame below is composed, not wireframed. The stage is the real still the app already falls back to (Art/direction/menu-stage-empty.png); the machines are fresh Blender renders of the real part library, lit with a stage rig and composited onto it; the type, the colours and the words are the ones in the build. Frames are 2622 x 1206 drawn at a third, so a size here is a point on the phone.

The drama is the face-off, and the thumb never leaves the bottom of the phone. Tinribs is the hero, lit and close, on the turntable. Clatterbox is a real machine on the far side of the stage under its own cold light, named in two small words, and the next fight is not a card describing it any more: it is a picture of the thing that is about to try to kill you, with one word on it. The four quiet tiles sit in a calm row exactly where a thumb rests in landscape. Nothing on this screen is a box with a bright edge, and nothing is still.

What moves

  • Camera orbits about 8 degrees over 24 seconds around the turntable and drifts back, never a cut.
  • Lights two spots sweep slowly out of phase; the warm key crosses the livery every few seconds.
  • Your machine idles: the flipper breathes against its stop, a spinner would spin up and throw sparks on a test fire every 20 seconds, a flamethrower would hold a pilot light.
  • Clatterbox rocks on its wheels and its saw ticks over, at half the volume and half the light.
  • Tiles do not animate on their own. Touch fills the tile solid and it stays filled while the screen changes.
  • Attract at 20 seconds idle the camera cuts to the broadcast package, the tiles fade out, the last fight replays bit exact with a title over it. Any touch brings the menu straight back.

Risk

The hero tile carries four pieces of information. If the opponent name is long it has to shrink; test with Penny Dread, the longest in the catalogue.
Cost from Godot. A second machine on the stage (the assembler already builds any opponent, so this is one more instance plus a light). An orbit camera path and two moving spots. A weapon idle state per weapon family (four families, not ten). An idle timer in Swift that asks the arena to play the attract package. A replay hook, which exists: the replay archive already plays bit exact. Not needed: any new art.

Option 2
M1 with the lights turned on. The layout the owner already chose, kept, but everything that made it feel inactive is fixed: the machine is big and lit instead of a dim silhouette at 28 per cent, the opponent is physically present on the far side instead of being a line of text, the card is lit rather than outlined, and the four modes are plain words in a column instead of boxes. It is the smallest change that answers the brief, and it is the cheapest to build.

What moves

  • Camera the same slow orbit as option 1, but framed wider so the right third stays quiet under the type.
  • Lights sweep; the cold spot on Clatterbox pulses very slightly, like a lamp on a dimmer.
  • Your machine the same weapon idle.
  • Opponent present but still, a machine waiting rather than warming up.
  • Tiles there is only one. The links highlight to full white on touch, no fill, no sweep.
  • Attract same as option 1 if you want it; this layout survives without it.

Risk

The right column still reads as a menu rather than a shelf of places to go, which is the thing Stradale avoids. On a 6.9 inch phone held in landscape the top right link is a stretch for the right thumb.
Cost from Godot. A second machine on the stage, an orbit camera, weapon idles. No idle timer and no replay hook unless attract is wanted. Swift side is a rearrangement of MenuView, about a day.

Option 3
The break: there is no menu until you ask for one. The app opens on the broadcast, not on the stage. A bit exact replay of your last fight, or a scripted exchange between you and the machine you are about to meet, runs full frame from the first frame of launch, with the fight card over it and one word to press. Touch anywhere and the replay dissolves to the stage and the tiles of option 1 rise into place. This is the opposite of my direction, which said keep the fight off the menu, and it is the most dramatic thing we could ship. It is also the riskiest.

What moves

  • Camera the broadcast camera from the fight, on a director's cut of three shots.
  • Lights the arena's, not the stage's, so the menu is a different room every Series.
  • Machines fighting. Everything moves, all the time.
  • Tiles absent until touched, then they arrive as the camera settles on the stage.
  • Attract is the default state rather than a timeout.

Risk, and it is real

  • A brand new player has no last fight and no opponent, so the first run needs a canned package. That is a piece of content we do not have.
  • The arena runs at full cost while the player reads a menu. That is a battery and a heat bill on every launch, and the menu is where players sit longest.
  • Text over a moving fight is the hardest legibility case in the game and the wash needed to fix it is the thing that will make it look flat.
  • Launch time goes up: the stage still can stand in, an arena cannot.
Cost from Godot. A menu mode for the fight scene with an AI director, a canned first run package, a launch path that loads an arena instead of the stage, and a performance budget nobody has measured. Two to three times option 1.

Whatever the owner picks for the menu, the garage is that screen with the fleet in the row. Same stage, same camera, same light, same tile row, same one lit tile. The only differences are the grade, which is warmer and has less dry ice so it reads as a working bay rather than a show, and the row, which is your machines rather than the modes. The four actions are words at the end of the row, in the order you are likely to want them, with the one that costs money in danger. The current garage is a stack of identical skewed panels; this is one picture and a shelf.

What moves

  • Camera the same orbit, slower, and it re-centres on whichever machine you select.
  • Lights a single overhead work lamp sweeps; no cold rim, so the room feels different without being a different room.
  • Your machine the same weapon idle, plus a damage read: a scorched panel and a wheel that sits wrong when it is carrying damage.
  • Tiles selecting a fleet tile swaps the machine on the turntable with a half second dissolve, and the tile fills solid.
  • Opponent not present. The garage is not a face-off.
Cost from Godot. Nothing new: it is the menu stage with a second grade and a machine swap, both of which the builder already does. Swift side is CareerGarageView redrawn against the same tile row component the menu uses, so the component is written once.

Option B
The garage is where numbers matter, so this one gives them a panel. The machine still owns the left of the frame and still turns, but the right is a single lit slab holding the whole build: the weight bar against the 90 kg limit, the armour by face, the free slot, the repair bill. The fleet drops to three small tiles under it and the actions are a quiet row on the left. It answers "what exactly am I driving" in one glance, which option A answers in two taps.

What moves

  • Camera the same orbit, but pushed left so the machine never runs under the panel.
  • Lights one work lamp, sweeping.
  • Your machine weapon idle plus the damage read.
  • Tiles the three fleet tiles swap the machine and every number in the panel at once.
  • The weight bar counts when a part changes, the one number that animates.

Risk

It is a panel, and section 8 allows at most two. It is one, so it is legal, but it is the thing that will drift back toward sixteen boxes the first time somebody needs to add a stat. It also breaks the promise that the garage is the menu again: two different layouts to build and to keep.
Cost from Godot. Identical to option A. All the extra work is Swift, and it is a second layout to maintain rather than one shared component.

Common groundWhat all five options share

Every option puts one lit machine on a turntable that never quite stops moving, gives the screen exactly one thing to press, and keeps the rest as words. None of them uses a line accent. The skewed cyan stroke on the leading edge of a button, the bar on a lower third and the coloured edge on a card are gone from all five, and nothing replaces them one for one. Emphasis now comes from four things that belong to a broadcast stage rather than a style sheet: light, the one tile that matters is lit from the top left as though a lamp on the gantry has found it, and everything else sits in shade; weight and size, the hero tile is taller than its neighbours and its headline is display italic at twice the label size; a real chamfer, a corner physically cut out of the slab rather than drawn on it, which is a shape, not a stroke; and fill, a touch fills the tile solid rather than sweeping a colour across its edge. Cyan survives as one thing only: a number or a word that leads somewhere, never an edge. If the owner picks one of these, docs/design-system.md sections 2 and 3 need rewriting to match: the lower third loses its 3 pt bar, the button loses its leading edge, and the panel keeps its one cut corner and loses its hairline.

They also share the same three layers behind the glass, which is the part that makes the screen feel alive rather than designed: layer one is your machine, idling, under lights that move; layer two is the next opponent, physically on the stage; layer three is the attract package after twenty seconds of being ignored. Options 1 and 2 differ only in how the words are arranged in front of those layers. Option 3 promotes layer three to the front door. The garage uses layers one and three and drops layer two.

Before anything is builtFive decisions

  1. Which menu, and is the attract package in or out? My recommendation is option 1 with attract in, because option 1 without attract is still a better screen than we have and attract can land a week later.
  2. Garage A or garage B? A costs less and keeps one component for both screens. B is the right answer only if the owner wants the build readable without opening the workshop.
  3. Is the opponent really on the stage, or only in the tile? A second machine on the menu stage is the single biggest cost in this document and the single biggest piece of drama. If the answer is no, both option 1 and option 2 still work with an empty far side and the picture in the tile.
  4. Workshop or Garage on the menu tile? The menu says Workshop, the career screen says Garage, and they are nearly the same screen. One word has to win before this is built.
  5. Does the livery stay the player's choice on the menu? Every frame here shows Tinribs in a warm orange so it reads apart from Clatterbox's blue. If a player paints their machine the same blue as the opponent, the face-off stops working. Either the opponent's light goes cold enough to separate them, or the game nudges the livery.
